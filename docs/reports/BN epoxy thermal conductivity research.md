# Let measurement physics shape the BN–epoxy dataset

The architecture of a source-verified k dataset for epoxy + h-BN / BNNS / AlN composites should start from one fact. For anisotropic platelet composites, **the measurement technique shifts the reported k by more than the instrument's stated uncertainty, and by roughly as much as typical model or ML error**. Vendor accuracy claims cluster at ±3–5%. Documented same-material differences are larger: about +6% for TPS against four other methods on PMMA, 15–50% for Hot Disk against laser flash on BN-type compounds, and more than 2× for ASTM D5470 against TPS on filled TIMs. Each instrument senses a different quantity. Isotropic-mode Hot Disk returns √(k_in·k_through). Laser flash on a disc returns through-plane k, with the Cp and density errors stacked on top. A D5470 rig returns an *apparent* through-plane k that includes contact resistance. The schema therefore has to treat method class, sensed direction and Cp/ρ provenance as first-class fields with explicit bias flags, not as free text.

On the physics side, one correctly coded Nan et al. (1997) function (anisotropic spheroids, an orientation average ⟨cos²θ⟩ and Kapitza resistance) reproduces Maxwell–Garnett and Hasselman–Johnson exactly as limiting cases, so it should be the core. Lewis–Nielsen (with φ_m fixed by filler shape) and a Foygel percolation term should supplement it above roughly 23 vol%, where Maxwell-type models underpredict severalfold. The main implementation risk is convention. Aspect ratio can be written t/D or D/t, and the Kapitza radius can be referenced to the semi-axis or the full thickness, which changes the fitted interfacial resistance by 2×.

On ML, no open curated composite-k dataset exists, and published R² values of 0.95–0.99 come from random splits. The one comparable study that held out whole sources saw R² collapse from 0.46 to 0.05. Grouped validation by paper, with physics baselines, is therefore mandatory.

## Four instrument families sense four different quantities

The polymer-composite literature relies on four measurement families, and each returns a physically different number. **Flash methods** (laser or xenon flash, LFA) measure thermal diffusivity through the thickness. A pulse heats the front face, an IR detector records the rear-face rise, and Parker's half-rise solution gives α = 1.38·L²/(π²·t½). Conductivity then follows only via k = α·ρ·Cp, with ρ and Cp "determined from separate experiments, which may result in the 'stack up' of uncertainties" ([Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.08469)). The governing standards are ASTM E1461 and ISO 22007-4. E1461's own scope is "primarily homogeneous isotropic solid materials", which a platelet-filled epoxy is not ([ASTM E1461-13(2022) listing](https://eshop.normservis.sk/norma/astm-e1461-13_2022-1.4.2022.html)).

**Plane- and line-source transient probes** fit k and α together from a heater's own temperature rise. This group includes the Hot Disk TPS (ISO 22007-2:2022, ASTM E3088-25), the Linseis transient hot bridge (developed with PTB as an "optimized hot-wire technique"), the hot wire and needle probe (ΔT = (q/4πk)·ln(t₂/t₁), which assumes an infinite isotropic medium), and the C-Therm MTPS, whose primary quantity is effusivity ([Hot Disk](https://www.hotdiskinstruments.com/products/measurement-modules/isotropic-standard-module/); [Linseis THB](https://www.linseis.com/en/methods/transient-hot-bridge-thb); [Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.08469)).

**Steady-state one-dimensional rigs** measure through-plane thermal resistance under clamping pressure. This family covers ASTM D5470, the E1530 guarded heat-flow meter (10–400 ×10⁻⁴ m²K/W, roughly 0.1–30 W/m·K) and the C177/C518 plates ([ASTM E1530-11](https://store.astm.org/e1530-11.html); [BSI D5470-17(2024)](https://knowledge.bsigroup.com/products/standard-test-method-for-thermal-transmission-properties-of-thermally-conductive-electrical-insulation-materials-1)).

**Thin-film and microscale methods** are rarely used on filled-epoxy discs. These are 3ω, TDTR/FDTR, lock-in IR thermography and photothermal radiometry. Their value to this project is conceptual: TDTR at low modulation frequency with a small spot also senses the geometric mean √(k_r·k_z). That makes the geometric-mean mixing seen in TPS a general feature of transient methods with radial spreading, not a quirk of one vendor ([Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.08469)).

Several instrument strings in Chinese-language papers decode cleanly into this taxonomy, though not all with equal confidence. A "Quick Thermal Conductivity Meter" is most often the Kyoto Electronics QTM series. This is a non-steady heat-wire probe working to JIS R2618, with a 60 s measurement. It requires samples of at least 100×50 mm, and the minimum thickness rises from 0.1 mm at 0.1 W/m·K to **5 mm at 5 W/m·K**. That requirement is easy to violate with the small cast discs typical of BN/epoxy papers ([KEM QTM principle](https://kem.kyoto/en/genri/quick-thermal-conductivity-meter); [KEM QTM-710/700](https://www.kem.kyoto/en/products/heat/qtm710-qtm700)). Chinese TPS-type "快速导热仪" instruments share the same name, so the attribution has to be resolved paper by paper from the model number.

"TC 3000/TC3000E" belongs to Xi'an XIATECH's transient hot-wire family ([CN101871900A](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN101871900A/en)). "DRL-III" is sold as a D5470 heat-flow tester ([Accio listing](https://www.accio.ai/find-product/en-12667-thermal-conductivity)). Its manufacturer remains unverified.

Two standards citations are traps. ASTM D7896 is a standard for *fluids* such as engine coolants ([ASTM D7896-19](https://store.astm.org/d7896-19.html)), and ASTM D7984 covers the *effusivity of fabrics* ([GlobalSpec D7984](https://standards.globalspec.com/std/14462589/d7984)). A paper that cites either for a solid epoxy disc is quoting the vendor's boilerplate, not the protocol it actually followed.

### Laser flash conductivity inherits every Cp, density and data-reduction choice

Because LFA measures α, the reported k is only as good as the independently sourced ρ and Cp, and Cp often dominates. An LFA study of Al/oxide and Al/Teflon composites estimated **7.66% uncertainty for α, 18.23% for heat capacity and 22.7% for k** ([TTU thesis](https://tdl-ir.tdl.org/handle/2346/ETD-TTU-2011-05-1406)). NETZSCH's own figures for the LFA 467 are ±3% for α and ±5% for Cp measured comparatively in the instrument ([NETZSCH spec](https://analyzing-testing.netzsch.com/_Resources/Persistent/f/9/0/2/f902a7aebecc31191c0f0d9d2ba6d9cb9f9d0e7b/Technical_Specifications_en_LFA_467_Hyperflash_LFA_467_HT_Hyperflash.pdf)). Careful three-scan sapphire DSC under ASTM E1269 reaches roughly 1.3–3.7% ([PTCA comparison](https://lhjywlfc.mat-test.com/en/article/id/7d3310bc-22ed-4779-be0a-baf08d1040d8)). Combining these vendor figures in quadrature puts k at about ±6% with comparative Cp and ±4–5% with DSC Cp.

A handbook or rule-of-mixtures Cp adds a bias of unknown size. A rule-of-mixtures density is worse at high loading: porosity lowers the true ρ, so using the theoretical density inflates k by roughly the void fraction.

Data reduction matters as much as inputs for thin, low-diffusivity, semi-transparent discs. The Cape–Lehman model accounts for finite pulse time, radiation and convection, and "is generally adopted" ([KNS 2009](https://kns.org/files/pre_paper/9/269박대규.pdf)). Coupled radiative–conductive models give "substantially different" results for NIR-transparent samples ([Luneva et al.](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2008.04079)). COMSOL modelling of graphite-coated thin samples found **deviations up to 60% for low-conductivity materials** ([Ioffe 2022](https://j.ioffe.ru/articles/viewPDF/53700)). NETZSCH recommends sputtered gold rather than graphite for polymer films of about 20 µm ([NETZSCH thin-film note](https://analyzing-testing.netzsch.com/pl/application-literature/thermal-diffusivity-of-extremely-thin-polymer-films)).

LFA is not intrinsically unreliable for polymers. On industrial rubber compounds it agreed with TPS and a guarded heat-flow meter ([AIP Conf. Proc. 2065](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/acp/article/2065/1/030041/837934/Thermal-conductivity-measurement-of-industrial)). The divergence appears specifically when fillers make the sample heterogeneous and anisotropic.

### Anisotropy turns method choice into a predictable bias

The most consequential bias is geometric-mean mixing. Hot Disk states that its isotropic module "deduced the geometrical average of the in-plane and through-plane Thermal Conductivity" ([Hot Disk](https://www.hotdiskinstruments.com/products/measurement-modules/isotropic-standard-module/)), and a peer-reviewed depth-profiling study confirms it ([RSI 94, 074902](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/rsi/article/94/7/074902/2901368/Thermal-conductivity-vs-depth-profiling-using-the)). The bias shows up even on a nominally isotropic polymer. In a five-method interlaboratory comparison on PMMA, conductivity and diffusivity results agreed within about ±10%, but TPS "repeatedly yielded 6% higher results than each other method" ([Rides et al. 2009](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229371097_Intercomparison_of_thermal_conductivity_and_thermal_diffusivity_methods_for_plastics); [Thermtest summary](https://thermtest.com/papers/intercomparison-of-thermal-conductivity-and-thermal-diffusivity-methods-for-plastics)).

The bias grows with filler anisotropy. A polymer/BN compound patent reports that Hot Disk on 4 mm injection-moulded plates reads **15–20% above LFA on 2 mm plates for isotropic fillers and up to 50% above for platelet fillers**. The same patent finds that through-plane k on 4 mm bars can exceed 2 mm values by 50% because of skin–core orientation ([US10328620B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10328620B2/en)).

The physics makes this correctable when the anisotropy ratio A = k_in/k_through is known: k_TPS/k_through = √A. Injection-moulded PVDF/BNNS gave k_in = 1.26 against k_through = 0.40 W/m·K, so A ≈ 3.2 ([PMC12845601](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12845601/)). An isotropic TPS reading on that material would sit about 1.8× above its LFA through-plane value, consistent in direction and magnitude with the patent's "up to 50%". Hot-pressed or filtered BNNS films with A of 5–20 would be overstated 2.2–4.5× relative to through-plane. Without a measured A, no correction is defensible, and the row must be labelled as a mixed-direction value.

Steady-state rigs bias in a different, application-dependent way. Wang (ORNL), with Delphi and GM co-authors, measured ten commercial filled TIMs. TPS repeatability was +0.18% to +1.58%, and filler-free vacuum grease agreed across methods. The D5470-rated materials, however, "showed much higher thermal conductivity", and TennMax was **more than double** its TPS value. In thin bond lines, where the gap approaches the filler size, the material becomes anisotropic and fillers bridge the gap. D5470 apparent k "is not a true material property, but rather, a material characteristic" ([OSTI 1570138](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1570138)).

D5470's own reproducibility is fragile. "Misalignments as low as 1% often go undetected", and no formal round-robin precision statement exists ([Electronics Cooling 2014](https://www.electronics-cooling.com/2014/02/towards-reproducible-astm-d5470-measurements-lower-cost/)). Bulk k and contact resistance separate only when the bond-line thickness is varied while the other conditions are held fixed ([ACS Material](https://www.acsmaterial.com/blog-detail/thermal-interface-material-measurement.html)). TPS has its own contact-resistance failure mode on thin specimens: errors of up to 26% on polyolefin films, cut to about 4% with a liquid interface ([Chalmers](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/547882/file/547882_Fulltext.pdf)), and a 40% overestimate on low-k samples before correction ([LBNL, arXiv 1910.01798](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.01798)).

Putting these together, the expected ordering for one in-plane-aligned BN/epoxy specimen is k_in > k_TPS-isotropic ≈ k_THB ≈ k_hot-wire > k_through(LFA) ≈ the intrinsic D5470 slope. A single-thickness D5470 value usually sits below the intrinsic k_through because contact resistance is lumped in. Filler bridging in thin bond lines can push it above. **No peer-reviewed study was found that measured the same BN/epoxy specimen by LFA, TPS and D5470.** The 15–50% figures rest on one patent.

### Packaging practice prefers D5470 because designers need interface resistance

Package designers care about the thermal resistance of the joint at application pressure. That resistance is the bond-line thickness divided by k, plus the two contact resistances, and only D5470 measures it directly. ASTM D5470 is "the only standard method to determine thermal contact resistance", most commercial TIM datasheets cite it, and the ORNL authors recommend TPS for material ranking and D5470 for application impedance ([OSTI 1570138](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1570138)).

JEDEC's JESD51-14 transient dual-interface method characterises *packages*. It measures junction-to-case R_θJC from two transients with and without a TIM, and was validated by a five-lab round robin ([JEDEC JESD51-14](https://www.jedec.org/standards-documents/docs/jesd51-14-0)). Its applicability beyond one-dimensional single-path packages was questioned in 2024 ([Electronics Cooling 2024](https://www.electronics-cooling.com/2024/04/the-applicability-of-jesd51-14-for-the-determination-of-junction-to-case-thermal-resistance/)). It defines no bulk-k method. For a course framed around semiconductor packaging, the implication is that a literature LFA k for BN/epoxy is an upper bound on in-package performance unless the bond line and contact resistances are small.

Calibration evidence is a usable quality signal. Pyroceram 9606 (IRMM BCR-724) is certified for k and α from room temperature to 1025 K ([JRC30964](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/handle/JRC30964)). Pyrex 7740 (BCR-039) is certified for k only, from −75 to 195 °C, and no current NIST diffusivity SRM exists ([ORNL](https://www.ornl.gov/publication/reference-materials-laser-and-xenon-flash-thermal-diffusivity-systems)). With k values of about 4 and 1.1 W/m·K, these two bracket the BN/epoxy range well, and PMMA anchors the neat-epoxy end.

## One Nan-1997 function underpins the physics module

The classical model zoo collapses into a small, testable core once its limits are understood. Maxwell (Maxwell–Garnett / Maxwell–Eucken) assumes non-interacting spheres. It is valid only in the dilute limit (≲10 vol% according to [Sarikhani et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.07635), under 25% according to [Pietrak & Wiśniewski](https://papers.itc.pw.edu.pl/index.php/JPT/article/view/463/637)), and its result does not depend on particle size. Hasselman–Johnson (1987) adds an interfacial conductance h_c and particle radius a, which makes k size-dependent through a·h_c. Tavangar et al. found it fails above an effective phase contrast of about 4, while a Bruggeman-type differential effective-medium (DEM) expression with interfacial resistance held across contrasts of 2–8 ([Pietrak & Wiśniewski 2015](https://papers.itc.pw.edu.pl/index.php/JPT/article/view/463/637)). Bruggeman/DEM "is considered the most accurate for high filler volume fractions" among classical effective-medium theories. The Every et al. (1992) form, (1 − φ)³ = (k_m/k)^((1+2α)/(1−α))·[(k − k_f(1−α))/(k_m − k_f(1−α))]^(3/(1−α)) with α = R_K·k_m/a, carries the Kapitza resistance into the asymmetric Bruggeman scheme ([same review](https://papers.itc.pw.edu.pl/index.php/JPT/article/view/463/637)).

Nan, Birringer, Clarke and Gleiter (1997) generalise all of this to arbitrary spheroids with Kapitza contact resistance ([DOI 10.1063/1.365209](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/effective-thermal-conductivity-of-particulate-composites-with-interfacial-thermal-resistance)). For a platelet with p = t/D < 1, the depolarization factors are L11 = p²/[2(p² − 1)] + p·cos⁻¹(p)/[2(1 − p²)^(3/2)] and L33 = 1 − 2L11. Each axis gets a "coated-particle" conductivity K_ii^c = k_p,ii/(1 + γ·L_ii·k_p,ii/k_m), with γ = (1 + 2p)·R_K·k_m/a3. The factors β_ii = (K_ii^c − k_m)/[k_m + L_ii(K_ii^c − k_m)] then enter orientation-averaged expressions for K11* and K33* weighted by ⟨cos²θ⟩, where θ is the angle between the measurement axis and the platelet normal ([Yuan et al. 2016](https://tpl.hust.edu.cn/__local/7/55/ED/AF9F0ECE565E8FEE45F7F9108FF_98BC40C5_1D81F7.pdf?e=.pdf)). h-BN's anisotropy is handled by inserting the in-plane k_p1 into K11^c and the through-plane k_p3 into K33^c ([Danayat et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.03527)).

Checking the coded formulas gives two identities that make Nan the right core. With p = 1 and R_K = 0, the random-orientation form reproduces Maxwell exactly. With p = 1 and R_K > 0, it reproduces the Hasselman–Johnson sphere formula, because k_f is simply replaced by k_f/(1 + k_f·R_K/a). One function therefore covers Maxwell, Hasselman–Johnson, Fricke/Hatta–Taya ellipsoids, platelets and needles. Only the "2 − φ{…}" sign in the K11* denominator reduces to Maxwell. The "+" in Danayat's extracted text is an extraction artefact.

Nan's model has one documented flaw that the project should disclose rather than hide. COMSOL single-particle simulations agree with Nan within 2% for spheres with R_K and for ellipsoids without it. For **ellipsoids with R_K the disagreement reaches about 100%**, and it grows as p departs from 1, because Nan implicitly assumes that the coated particle is an ellipsoid with the bare particle's aspect ratio ([Berne, COMSOL 2015](https://www.comsol.com/paper/download/288771/berne_paper.pdf)). Berne himself judged the practical consequences minimal ([Berne poster](https://www.comsol.com/paper/download/288781/berne_poster.pdf)).

### Lewis–Nielsen and percolation terms are needed above about 23 vol%

The decisive BN/epoxy benchmark is Kargar et al. They measured epoxy with randomly oriented graphene or h-BN up to 45 vol%. k rose roughly linearly to about 23 vol% for h-BN and then super-linearly, reaching an **enhancement of about 24× at 45 vol%**. Maxwell–Garnett with boundary resistance worked for graphene only below about 10 vol%. Geometric-mean fits were not physically sensible. **Lewis–Nielsen fitted best over the entire range**: with φ_m fixed at 0.52 (3D random), the h-BN fit returned A ≈ 31 and an *apparent* k_f of only about 16 W/m·K. The authors attribute the low apparent k_f to cross-plane conduction between overlapping flakes and to filler–filler boundary resistance ([Kargar et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.03698)). Sarikhani et al. found the same ranking on MWCNT/PP: only Lewis–Nielsen fitted relatively well among Lichtenecker, Agari–Uno, Russell, Cheng–Vachon and Lewis–Nielsen ([Sarikhani et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.07635)).

Lewis–Nielsen has two limits that matter in practice. First, its maximum-packing parameter is not universal: across 17 experimental particulate-composite datasets the fitted φ_m ranged from 0.32 to 1.0 ([Composites Part A 2008](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1359835X08000389)). Second, it "becomes unstable" above about 40 vol%, where its denominator approaches zero as φ → φ_m ([Pietrak & Wiśniewski](https://papers.itc.pw.edu.pl/index.php/JPT/article/view/463/637)).

For anisotropic BN, practitioners switch parameter sets by direction. One alumina/BN/epoxy study used k_f = 400 W/m·K with A = 10 for in-plane conduction and k_f = 2 W/m·K with A = 0.5 for through-plane ([PMC6523155](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6523155/)). Agari–Uno (log k = φ·C2·log k_f + (1 − φ)·log(C1·k_m)) still appears in BN and AlN papers as a descriptive fit, for example h-BN in cyanate-ester/epoxy ([Macromol. Res. 2018](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s13233-018-6090-4)) and 3D-AlN/epoxy ([Wang et al. 2023](https://fhclxb.buaa.edu.cn/article/doi/10.13801/j.cnki.fhclxb.20220905.002)). Its parameters carry little physics.

Percolation terms handle the super-linear regime, but they need to be used with caution. Jang et al. built a Nan + Foygel hybrid, k = k_m + K0·[(φ − φ_c)/(1 − φ_c)]^β, with φ_c = 23 vol%, K0 = 7 W/m·K and β = 0.35. It predicts 6.55–10.93 W/m·K for h-BN/BNNT at 31–50 vol% against 5.01 W/m·K for h-BN alone at 49.45 vol%. The paper's reported filler–filler contact resistance (1.20×10⁶ K/W), however, does not follow from its own inputs: recomputing gives about 8×10⁴ K/W ([Jang et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12464805)). Sarikhani et al. add the conceptual warning. Classical effective-medium models become non-linear at higher φ even without a network, so super-linearity alone does not prove thermal percolation. Thermal composites are a smeared "Type III" case whose crossover width scales with the conductivity ratio ([Sarikhani et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.07635)).

### Fitted interfacial resistance absorbs everything the model leaves out

Back-fitted Kapitza resistances for h-BN/epoxy span three orders of magnitude, which shows they are apparent quantities. Yuan et al. fitted 360×10⁻⁹ m²K/W for random h-BN at 10, 15 and 20 vol%. With combined mechanical and magnetic alignment the value fell to 11×10⁻⁹ at 10 vol%, then rose to 186 and 232×10⁻⁹ at 15 and 20 vol% ([Yuan et al. 2016](https://tpl.hust.edu.cn/__local/7/55/ED/AF9F0ECE565E8FEE45F7F9108FF_98BC40C5_1D81F7.pdf?e=.pdf)). Another effective-medium study found **2.44×10⁻⁶ m²K/W for unmodified h-BN falling to 4.73×10⁻⁷ after surface modification**, about a fivefold drop ([Materials Reports 2023](https://www.mater-rep.com/EN/10.11896/cldb.21110039)). Molecular dynamics for ideal hBN-nanosheet/epoxy interfaces gives only (2.42–3.62)×10⁻⁹ m²K/W ([Yang et al. 2020](https://lammps.org/abstracts/abstract.20131.html)). Liquid-crystalline epoxy lowers the measured interfacial resistance by 15–65% ([Pang et al. 2025](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/reducing-the-interfacial-thermal-resistance-between-liquid-crysta/)).

The one to three orders of magnitude between model-fitted and MD values mean a fitted R_K soaks up voids, agglomeration, errors in the assumed k_p⊥, and Nan's own coating approximation. The project should report it as a per-paper diagnostic, not as interface physics. Two consequences follow for the code. First, the Kapitza radius a_K = R_K·k_m is about 20 nm at 10⁻⁷ m²K/W in epoxy, so few-nanometre BNNS are far more R_K-sensitive than micron platelets. Second, Foygel contact resistances reported for AlN/epoxy (2.704×10⁵ K/W for 3D networks and 4.019×10⁵ K/W for random dispersions) are per contact in K/W, not per area ([Wang et al. 2023](https://fhclxb.buaa.edu.cn/article/doi/10.13801/j.cnki.fhclxb.20220905.002)). They must never share a column with R_K.

A worked evaluation of the cited platelet equations shows how strongly R_K throttles even micron flakes. Take D = 5 µm, t = 250 nm, k∥ = 400 and k⊥ = 5 W/m·K. At R_K = 10⁻⁷ m²K/W, the coated-particle conductivities are K11^c ≈ 28.6 and K33^c ≈ 0.99 W/m·K. At 10⁻⁸ they are about 174 and 3.55 W/m·K. The first case is the same order as Kargar's apparent k_f of 16 W/m·K.

### Inputs and conventions decide whether fitted parameters are comparable

Intrinsic filler values should be exposed as parameters and tested for sensitivity, not hard-coded. TDTR on natural bulk h-BN gives **420 ± 46 W/m·K in-plane and 4.8 ± 0.6 W/m·K through-plane**, against first-principles values of 537 and 4.1 ([Jiang et al., arXiv 1805.00564](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.00564)). Monoisotopic ¹⁰B h-BN reaches 585 W/m·K ([ORNL listing](https://www.ornl.gov/publication/modulating-thermal-conductivity-hexagonal-boron-nitride-controlled-boron-isotope)). For AlN, the Ioffe database lists 285 W/m·K as the maximum measured at 300 K ([Ioffe](https://www.ioffe.ru/SVA/NSM/Semicond/AlN/thermal.html)). About 319 W/m·K is quoted as the theoretical intrinsic value, with polycrystalline ceramics ranging from 17 to 285 ([Franco Júnior & Shanafield](https://www.scielo.br/j/ce/a/7nmz9P3NBmRXqkzjGfJTMnL/?lang=en)). Published modellers variously input 300, 390, or 400/2 W/m·K for BN. An ML study found that filler k has "a relatively minor impact" ([Liu et al. 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352492824010973)), which is consistent with effective-medium saturation at k_f/k_m ≈ 1000.

Loading conversion uses φ = (w/ρ_f)/[w/ρ_f + (1 − w)/ρ_m]. Kargar et al. refined this by regressing measured composite density against φ until self-consistent, arriving at ρ(h-BN) = 2.07 and ρ(epoxy) = 1.17 g/cm³ ([Kargar et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.03698)).

Three convention traps follow from the formulas.

The first is aspect ratio. Nan, Yuan and Danayat define p = t/D < 1 for platelets, while many BN papers quote D/t > 1. Berne's text even labels p > 1 "oblate", although the limits (L33 → 1 as p → 0) only work the other way.

The second is the Kapitza radius. Nan's α references the semi-axis a3 = t/2, whereas Yuan et al. write γ = (1 + 2p)·R_b·k_m/t with the full thickness. An R_b fitted under Yuan's convention is therefore about twice the Nan-consistent value.

The third is the packing function in Lewis–Nielsen: variants exist and must be flagged.

No maintained Python package implements this thermal-composite set. The nearest tools are pyrrhenius (Bruggeman for electrical mixing), hashin_shtrikman_mp (HS bounds) and pyhom (2D full-field homogenisation), which serve as cross-checks ([pyrrhenius](https://pyrrhenius.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_modules/pyrrhenius/mixing.html); [JOSS hashin_shtrikman_mp](https://www.theoj.org/joss-papers/joss.08412/10.21105.joss.08412.pdf); [pyhom](https://pypi.org/project/pyhom/)).

## Published R² near 0.98 collapses when whole papers are held out

No open, curated dataset of filled-polymer composite k with composition and processing metadata exists. The large polymer databases barely cover composites. PoLyInfo has thermal-conductivity entries for **only 84 homopolymers** against more than 8,000 with Tg, and it prohibits bulk download ([Huang & Ju 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.15887)).

Polymer Scholar is the largest LLM-extracted resource. It holds 5,574 GPT-3.5 and 10,300 MaterialsBERT full-text thermal-conductivity records drawn from about 2.4 M articles, produced in roughly a month for about US$1,200. Composites are its known weak spot: MaterialsBERT "often extracted redundant values" for blends and composites, GPT-3.5 missed fillers in complex sentences, and Tg extraction F1 was only 0.67 ([Gupta et al. 2024](https://ramprasad.mse.gatech.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/s43246-024-00708-9.pdf)). A Kaggle "Polymer Composite Properties Dataset" of 12,700 × 35 has undocumented provenance, which is implausible for hand-curated experimental data ([Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/colabsss/polymer-composite-properties-dataset)).

Paper-specific literature sets run from 93 to about 450 rows. Examples are 370 samples for carbon-filled composites ([JMST 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1005030226000046)) and 364 for cryogenic epoxy composites ([Springer 2026](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-26376-6_5)). The one larger set, 1,774 points, appears to come from industrial compounders ([Han et al. 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mgea.70027)). By comparison, the project's 260 rows from 95 papers are typical in size but unusually diverse per row.

The accuracy claims in this literature are almost uniformly random-split. Liu et al. report Pearson r = 0.981 with gradient-boosted trees. They also introduced topological polar surface area as a descriptor for surface modifiers ([Liu et al. 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352492824010973)). A physics-constrained co-training ANN for BN composites reports **R² = 0.982 and MAE = 0.045 W/m·K** ([Energy and AI 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666546825001958)). Han et al. report an average R² of 0.95 across seven properties. A PEG-nanocomposite set of 229 samples shows the typical gap between train R² of 0.9966 and test R² of 0.9158 ([An. Acad. Bras. Ciênc. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1590/0001-3765202520241430)). Simulation-trained models look better still: a CNN reached about 1.9% RMSE against 7.3% for Maxwell–Eucken and 18.3% for Bruggeman ([Wei et al. 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0017931018317423)). Those microstructures are synthetic and in-distribution.

The closest analogue with honest validation tells a different story. Alqurashi and Khan modelled 150 nano-enhanced phase-change-material measurements from 21 studies, with the target log10(k_eff/k_base). Under "preprocessing-leakage-free" 10-fold CV, gradient boosting reached **R² = 0.46 ± 0.56**, and SVR and the neural network had negative CV R² despite high training scores. **Leave-one-source-out R² was about 0.05.** The authors conclude that the model suits "screening within represented material families" but not quantitative prediction for unseen sources ([Alqurashi & Khan 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-68034-3)). A 47-study mycelium meta-dataset reports the same "limited cross-study predictability" ([Materials & Design 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matdes.2026.116566)).

The general best-practice literature points the same way. Cluster-based splits cut materials-ML accuracy 2–4× relative to random CV ([Meredig et al. 2018](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2018/me/c8me00012c), via [Artrith et al. 2021](https://www.coudert.name/papers/10.1038_s41557-021-00716-z.pdf)). Leakage has affected 294 papers across 17 fields ([Kapoor & Narayanan 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10499856)). scikit-learn's own guidance warns that grouped samples break the i.i.d. assumption and provides GroupKFold, LeaveOneGroupOut and Pipeline-contained preprocessing ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/cross_validation.html)). The Artrith checklist asks authors to flag multi-source data, to justify whether the split "mimic[s] anticipated real-world application", and to compare against 1-nearest-neighbour, random-forest and predict-the-mean baselines ([Artrith et al.](https://www.coudert.name/papers/10.1038_s41557-021-00716-z.pdf)). K-means leave-cluster-out CV has its own reproducibility problems, which is an argument for grouping by a natural key such as DOI ([Durdy et al. 2022](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2022/dd/d2dd00039c)). Taken together, the 0.95–0.99 figures in the composite-k literature are upper bounds on within-paper interpolation along a loading series.

Physics-informed design is the most credible way to recover accuracy at this data scale, although the evidence comes from adjacent problems. Zhang and Ling showed that adding a "crude estimation of property" to the feature space raises small-data accuracy "without the cost of higher DoF", and one of their three case studies was lattice thermal conductivity ([npj Comput. Mater. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41524-018-0081-z)). Monotonic Gaussian processes reduce posterior variance at a small accuracy cost. Their monotonic effect "starts fading away" outside the training data, so they help interpolation, not extrapolation ([Tran et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.00628)). Gradient-boosting libraries support monotone constraints directly ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/1.3/_sources/auto_examples/ensemble/plot_monotonic_constraints.rst.txt)). For h-BN/polyimide, Ding et al. paired an iterative effective-medium model with an ANN ([NWPU](https://pure.nwpu.edu.cn/en/publications/thermal-conductivity-of-polydisperse-hexagonal-bnpolyimide-compos)). **No published study does residual learning on an effective-medium baseline for experimental polymer-composite k with grouped validation.** The project would be filling a real gap, and the benefit is not yet demonstrated.

Uncertainty quantification has the same small-data caveats. Raw bootstrap-ensemble spread "is not an accurate estimate of uncertainty", but simple calibration fixes much of the gap ([Palmer et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41524-022-00794-8)). Ensembles are not always the best UQ choice ([Varivoda et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0133528)). Evaluation should report both calibration and sharpness ([Tran et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1088/2632-2153/ab7e1a)). Conformal coverage guarantees weaken with small calibration sets ([arXiv 2512.04566](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.04566v2)), and the best nonconformity score depends on the data ([arXiv 2410.09894](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.09894)). On metadata, the field is weak. Most ML papers use volume fraction as the key variable but rarely document how they converted wt%, treated direction or encoded measurement method, and a 2025 review names inconsistent test methods and incomplete metadata as core barriers to cross-study polymer ML ([Zhao, Chen, Liu 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13494)).

## 2025–26 API changes and LLM evidence reshape the extraction pipeline

The discovery layer changed materially in the past year. **OpenAlex** moved to API keys and usage pricing, announced in its 24 Feb 2026 post. A free key gets $1/day. Singleton DOI lookups are free, list/filter calls cost $0.10 per 1,000, searches $1 per 1,000, and PDF or TEI downloads $10 per 1,000 ([OpenAlex blog](https://blog.openalex.org/author/jason)). The current documentation gives 10 req/s without a key, 30 with one and 50 on an annual plan, and says a key is required for cursor paging past the first page ([OpenAlex rate limits](https://help.openalex.org/how-to-use-the-api/rate-limits-and-authentication)). Sources conflict on how much keyless access remains ($0.10/day or $0.01/day) ([OpenAlex example costs](https://help.openalex.org/access/example-costs/)). OpenAlex also replaced its backend: the "Walden" rewrite went live on 3 Nov 2025, adding 190M works, so cached pre-November queries are stale ([OpenAlex blog](https://blog.openalex.org/author/jason)). A new content endpoint serves PDFs and GROBID TEI for tens of millions of OA works at $0.01 each, which caps a free key at about 100 files a day ([OpenAlex get-content](https://docs.openalex.org/how-to-use-the-api/get-content)).

**Crossref** revised its limits on 1 Dec 2025, for the first time since 2013. Public list queries are now 1 req/s at concurrency 1, and the polite pool (with `mailto`) allows 3 req/s at concurrency 3 ([Crossref blog](https://crossref.org/blog/announcing-changes-to-rest-api-rate-limits/)). Crossref's text-and-data-mining guidance is explicit that "the absence of a license does not mean that the full text can be used without one" ([Crossref TDM](https://crossref.org/documentation/retrieve-metadata/rest-api/text-and-data-mining-for-researchers)).

Europe PMC needs no key. Its REST search returns per-record `isOpenAccess`, `license` and `fullTextUrlList` fields, and `/{PMCID}/fullTextXML` returns JATS with `<permissions>`, `<table-wrap>` and `<fig>` elements ([Europe PMC search](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1038/s41467-024-45563-x&format=json&resultType=core); [fullTextXML](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10869356/fullTextXML)). Unpaywall still asks for ≤100,000 calls/day with an `email` parameter. It now runs on OpenAlex infrastructure, which changed `oa_status` for about 10% of works and deprecated `oa_locations.evidence` ([Unpaywall](https://unpaywall.org/products/api); [OpenAlex blog](https://blog.openalex.org/major-update-to-unpaywall-database)). Semantic Scholar's unauthenticated pool is shared across all users, and new keys start at 1 req/s ([Semantic Scholar API](https://www.semanticscholar.org/product/api)). arXiv allows one request every 3 seconds. Its metadata is CC0, but full text may not be re-served ([arXiv ToU](https://info.arxiv.org/help/api/tou.html)).

For parsing, structured XML beats every PDF tool, and among PDF tools the table gaps are large. On OmniDocBench, table TEDS was **MinerU 78.6, Mathpix 77.0, Qwen2-VL-72B 76.8, GPT-4o 72.0, Marker 67.6 and Nougat 39.9** ([OmniDocBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.07626)). In a ten-tool benchmark of academic PDFs, GROBID was best for metadata and references, and table performance "is much lower than for other content elements" ([Meuschke et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.09957v1)). Docling's code is MIT-licensed and it needs Python ≥3.10 ([Docling](https://github.com/docling-project/docling)). Marker's code is Apache-2.0, but its weights are free only for research and for organisations under $5M ([Marker](https://github.com/datalab-to/marker)). Community tests flag merged multi-row headers as a Docling weakness ([CodeSOTA](https://www.codesota.com/ocr/docling-vs-mineru)). ChatExtract's authors counted broken XML-to-text tables as errors "because humans could not extract them either", a reminder that the source format often limits accuracy before the extractor does ([Polak & Morgan 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10882009)).

LLM extraction works when it is verified. **ChatExtract with GPT-4 reached 90.8% precision and 87.7% recall** on material–value–unit triplets. Without its doubt-inducing follow-up questions, precision dropped to 42.7% while recall rose to 98.9%, so the verification step is what buys precision. Table datapoints reached 91% precision and 89% recall, and rule-based ChemDataExtractor 2 managed only 57% precision and 31% recall on the same task ([Polak & Morgan 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10882009)). Gupta et al. passed only about 11% of paragraphs through a synonym heuristic, then about 3% through an NER filter requiring material, property and value together. They credit this filter with preventing hallucinated values ([Gupta et al.](https://ramprasad.mse.gatech.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/s43246-024-00708-9.pdf)). MaTableGPT reached a table F1 of up to 96.8% using table splitting and follow-up filtering ([MaTableGPT](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12021050/)). On polymer-composite tables specifically, the best setup gave a multimodal model the table *image*, scoring 0.910 on composition extraction ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10539623-how-well-do-large-language-models-understand-tables-materials-science)). Human-in-the-loop pre-annotation cut annotation time per abstract by 57% at 300 training examples, while models trained on fewer than 50 examples often emitted invalid JSON ([Dagdelen et al. 2024](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10869356/fullTextXML)). No published extraction benchmark exists for the multi-field record this project needs (filler, loading and basis, k, method, direction).

Figure digitization needs its own uncertainty model. PlotExtract reports more than 90% precision, about 90% recall and x/y errors of about 5% on extractable two-axis plots. It has the LLM regenerate the plot so the copy can be diffed visually against the original ([PlotExtract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.12326)). Workflows matter here: LLMs given MatDeplot-reconstructed curves reach **4.2% median relative error, against 54.3% for VLMs reading the chart image directly** ([MatDeplot, ICML 2026](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/73589)). WebPlotDigitizer v5.2 has an AGPL-3.0 frontend, but its "AI Assist" cloud components are closed ([WPD GitHub](https://github.com/automeris-io/WebPlotDigitizer)). Studies of human digitizers find that reliability depends on the individual graph more than on the software ([Moeyaert et al. summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/assessment-research/reliability--validity--and-usability-of-data-extraction-programs-for-s/)).

For storage and validation, pandera (MIT) supports pandas and polars, and `pandera.pandas` is now the recommended import ([pandera](https://github.com/unionai-oss/pandera)). pint raises `OffsetUnitCalculusError` on °C arithmetic, which stops silent temperature errors ([pint](https://pint.readthedocs.io/en/stable/user/nonmult.html)). Frictionless Data Package v2.0 shipped in June 2024, but as of January 2026 the Python and R libraries "could only handle version 1" ([Frictionless call](https://frictionlessdata.io/blog/2026/01/23/monthly-community-call/)). ASReview LAB 2.0 offers auditable active-learning screening, with a 24.1% loss reduction over v1 on the SYNERGY benchmark ([ASReview 2.0](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12416088)).

## Design implications for the toolkit

### Measurement vocabulary should carry its own bias flags

The 68 free-text method strings should map onto a controlled `method_class`. Each class carries a default sensed direction, a primary measured quantity and bias flags, so that downstream analysis can stratify or correct rather than average. The default uncertainties below are judgement values synthesised from the vendor and interlaboratory figures above. They are not standard values. They should apply only when a paper reports none, and they are random components: the direction bias is modelled separately.

| method_class | Trigger strings (examples) | Primary quantity | Default `k_direction` | Default 1σ | Flags raised automatically |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `LFA_through` | flash, LFA 447/457/467, NanoFlash, HyperFlash, DLF, E1461, ISO 22007-4, "k = α·ρ·Cp" | Diffusivity | through-plane | ±5% (DSC Cp) / ±8–10% (assumed Cp, ρ) | `assumed_cp_rho`, `parker_only`, `graphite_on_thin_film` |
| `LFA_inplane` | in-plane holder, ring mask, stacked and cut cross-section | Diffusivity | in-plane | ±5–8% | `assumed_cp_rho` |
| `TPS_isotropic` | Hot Disk, TPS 2500 S, transient plane source, ISO 22007-2, Chinese TPS clones | k + α | mixed_geometric | ±5% | `mixed_direction`, `thin_TPS` |
| `TPS_anisotropic` | anisotropic module, separate k_in and k_through reported | k + α + ρCp | in-plane and through-plane | ±5–7% | `needs_rhoCp` |
| `THB` | transient hot bridge, Linseis THB-100 | k + α | mixed_geometric (assumed) | ±5% | `mixed_direction` |
| `HOT_WIRE` | hot wire, needle probe, KEM QTM, "quick thermal conductivity meter", TC 3000, D5930 | k | mixed | ±5–7% | `mixed_direction`, `qtm_thickness_check` |
| `MTPS` | C-Therm, TCi, Trident, effusivity | Effusivity | mixed_unknown | ±5–7% | `mixed_direction` |
| `SS_TIM_D5470` | D5470, DRL-III, plane heat flow, thermal resistance tester | Impedance | through-plane (apparent) | ±10% | `contact_included` unless multi-thickness |
| `SS_GHFM_E1530` | guarded heat-flow meter, DTC-300, E1530 | Resistance | through-plane | ±5–8% | `contact_included` |
| `SS_HFM_GHP` | C177, C518, guarded hot plate, ISO 8302 | k | through-plane | ±5% | — |
| `THIN_FILM`, `IR_THERMO`, `PHOTOTHERMAL` | 3ω, TDTR, FDTR; lock-in IR, Ångström; PTR, ISO 22007-3 | Varies | per configuration | per paper | `local_probe` |
| `UNSPECIFIED` | instrument model only | — | unknown | ±10% | `method_unresolved` |

Five schema rules follow.

First, the `k_direction` enum needs a fourth value, `mixed_geometric`, rather than forcing TPS, THB and hot-wire rows into "in-plane" or "through-plane". A derived through-plane estimate k_TPS/√A should be computed only when the same paper reports A. Otherwise the row stays flagged and unconverted.

Second, every LFA row needs `cp_source` ∈ {DSC, LFA-comparative, literature/rule-of-mixtures, unknown} and `rho_source` ∈ {Archimedes/pycnometer, rule-of-mixtures, unknown}.

Third, D5470-family rows need optional `pressure`, `bond_line_thickness` and `multi_thickness` fields, because only multi-thickness data separates bulk k from contact resistance.

Fourth, citations of ASTM D7896 or D7984 for solid samples should raise a `standard_mismatch` flag. JESD51 R_θJC or R_θJA values belong in a separate package-level table, never in the k column.

Fifth, a `calibration_reported` boolean should record Pyroceram, Pyrex or PMMA checks. Together with the direction and Cp flags, it gives every row a transparent quality grade.

### Physics models should share one core and explicit conventions

The module should be organised around a single `nan1997()` implementation of depolarization factors, coated-particle conductivities and orientation-averaged K11*/K33*. Thin wrappers then expose the classical names: Maxwell, Hasselman–Johnson and Fricke. Separate implementations cover the Wiener and Hashin–Shtrikman bounds, symmetric Bruggeman, DEM and Every (solved with a bracketed root finder), Lewis–Nielsen, Agari–Uno and Foygel. The equations for Hashin–Shtrikman, Bruggeman, Agari, Cheng–Vachon and Russell came from secondary or textbook forms checked only by limits. They need verification against the originals before they are relied on.

Unit tests should assert the identities that make the module trustworthy: Nan(p = 1, R_K = 0) equals Maxwell; Nan(p = 1, R_K) equals Hasselman–Johnson; the HS lower bound equals Maxwell; Lewis–Nielsen tends to the parallel bound as A → ∞ and to the series bound as A → 0; and Every(α = 0, k_f → ∞) equals (1 − φ)⁻³.

| Parameter | Convention to enforce | Why |
|---|---|---|
| φ | Volume fraction 0–1; store `loading_basis_reported` and `vol_derived` flag plus the ρ values used | 191 rows are wt%; a ±0.1 g/cm³ ρ error shifts φ by ~2–3% relative |
| p | p = t/D (< 1 platelet, > 1 needle); store reported D/t separately | Limits only work with t/D; papers mix both |
| Kapitza radius | a_K = R_K·k_m referenced to the semi-axis a3 = t/2; store `rk_convention` with any literature R_K | Yuan's full-thickness convention doubles fitted R_K |
| R_K | m²K/W; fit in log space with a log-uniform prior of 10⁻⁹–10⁻⁵; values > 10⁻⁶ flagged as dispersion or input problems | Fitted values are apparent; MD floor ≈ 3×10⁻⁹ |
| Foygel R_c | K/W per contact, separate column | Not dimensionally comparable with R_K |
| ⟨cos²θ⟩ | θ from platelet normal to measurement axis; random 1/3; through-plane of in-plane-aligned film ≈ 1; vertically aligned ≈ 0; also store Herman's S = (3⟨cos²θ⟩ − 1)/2 | Trades off against R_K if both are fitted |
| Lewis–Nielsen φ_m | Fixed by shape class (0.52 for 3D-random platelets, following Kargar); fit only A and apparent k_f; flag the ψ variant | A, φ_m and k_f are degenerate when fitted together; φ_m ranges 0.32–1.0 across systems |
| Filler k | Parameters with defaults: h-BN 420 / 4.8, AlN 285 (range 150–320); run sensitivity sweeps | Modellers' inputs vary from 300 to 400 |

The fitting strategy follows from the evidence. Every row first goes through a bounds check against Wiener and HS limits, using in-plane k_f for the upper bound and through-plane k_f for the lower, to catch wt%/vol% and unit errors. Below about 20 vol%, fit Nan with one R_K per paper. Between 20 and 60 vol%, compare Lewis–Nielsen against a Nan + Foygel hybrid with φ_c ≈ 0.20–0.25, using AIC or BIC because the models carry one to three free parameters. Rows from aligned films and 3D BN networks can sit at 20–47 W/m·K and need separate treatment. They should be compared only against aligned-orientation Nan or parallel-bound expressions, never against random-orientation models.

### ML validation should predict performance on a paper not yet read

The primary target should be log10(k), with log10(k/k_m) as the main alternative where the same paper reports k_m (127 rows). The ratio cancels part of the lab- and method-level offset, as in the only honestly validated analogue. The primary metric should be GroupKFold by DOI, repeated with reshuffled group assignments, or leave-one-paper-out. Results should be reported as MAE in log10 units, RMSE, R² and median fold-wise factor error. Random K-fold should be reported only to show the leakage gap. Leave-filler-family-out (train on BN, test on AlN or hybrids) and a time split serve as extrapolation checks.

Every model should beat five baselines: the training mean, the matrix-class mean, 1-NN on (filler, vol%), pure Maxwell or Lewis–Nielsen, and linear log-k against vol%. All imputation, scaling, target encoding and feature selection must live inside the fold pipeline. Papers contributing many rows should be down-weighted so that a few long loading series do not dominate.

Model capacity should match roughly 95 independent groups. Suitable choices are regularised linear or GAM-style models with effective-medium features, a Gaussian process with an effective-medium mean, and shallow gradient boosting with a monotone-increasing constraint on loading, plus random forest as the robust reference. Deep nets and SVR overfit in the closest analogue and should be avoided. Effective-medium predictions should enter three ways: as baselines to beat, as Zhang–Ling-style features, and as an offset for residual learning. Interface, alignment and processing effects then become what the ML learns.

Intervals should come from calibrated ensembles or a GP, wrapped in group-aware cross-conformal (CV+) over paper folds so that coverage reflects new-paper uncertainty. An honest outcome on unseen papers is probably an MAE of 0.15–0.30 in log10 k, a factor of about 1.4–2, with intervals spanning a factor of 2–3. Intervals much narrower than the grouped-CV error are miscalibrated. This is an inference from the Alqurashi–Khan and Meredig results, not a published BN/epoxy number.

Because method is confounded with paper, a per-method residual table is more informative than a learned method coefficient, and it is exactly the analysis a characterization-techniques course should showcase. An Artrith-style checklist and a Kapoor-style model info sheet should accompany the results. They should state cleaning counts, conversion rules, imputation rates (filler size is known for only 71 of 260 rows, alignment for 29) and the split rationale.

### Tooling should respect 2026 rate, cost and licence constraints

Discovery should use OpenAlex with a free key held in an environment variable, keeping search and list calls to a few dozen and resolving candidates by free singleton DOI lookups. Crossref should run in the polite pool at concurrency ≤3 for authoritative metadata and TDM licence fields. Unpaywall resolves a legal OA copy, and Europe PMC supplies JATS. All clients share a per-host token-bucket limiter that honours `Retry-After` and backs off on 429. Every row records the API, access date and OpenAlex data version, so post-Walden changes stay traceable. Licence becomes a gating field: no licence means do not mine, per Crossref's guidance. Title and abstract screening should run in ASReview, simulated first with the 95 known-relevant papers, and logged PRISMA-style.

Retrieval and parsing follow a fixed source ladder: Europe PMC JATS (`<table-wrap>` with captions and footnotes, where units hide), then OpenAlex GROBID TEI (about 100 files a day free), then OA PDFs through Docling, with Marker or MinerU as a second opinion on table pages and flagging of cells where they disagree.

Extraction keeps a deterministic pre-filter for "thermal conductivity", "W/(m·K)"-style units and filler names. The LLM extractor must emit pydantic-validated JSON that includes a verbatim `source_quote`, which is string-matched against the stored artifact. ChatExtract-style follow-up questions ("wt% or vol%?", "composite or neat matrix?", "which direction?") recover precision. The 260 hand-verified rows act as the CI gold set, scored per field. Polymer Scholar's k records serve only as a recall check, and anything taken from them is re-verified against the original paper.

Digitized points carry the tool and version, axis calibration pixels, log/linear flags, source resolution, pixel uncertainty converted to W/m·K, and a repeat-digitization difference. A VLM (PlotExtract-style, with plot regeneration) acts as a second opinion that is auto-accepted only within pixel uncertainty. Text and table values take precedence over figure values whenever both exist.

The storage layer must fix the existing pipeline's known defects: the append-after-reject bug, the missing idempotency key, non-atomic writes and the unused normalizer. Validators should return accepted and rejected partitions, with rejected rows sent to a quarantine file and a test asserting they never reach output. A deterministic `record_id` hashed from DOI, source location, material descriptor, loading and basis, rounded k and temperature supports upserts and a CI duplicate check. Writes go to a temp file followed by `os.replace`. Normalisation lives in a pydantic model validator using pint with a unit whitelist, so no row exists without `kappa_W_per_mK`. pandera handles table-level checks.

Publication should use a v1-compatible Frictionless `datapackage.json`. Raw artifacts should be versioned with DVC or Git LFS only where the licence permits redistribution; otherwise the dataset stores only DOI plus SHA-256 hash. Recorded HTTP fixtures keep CI off live APIs.

## Conclusion

The central lesson is that in this domain the *label* is noisier than the features, and the physics of characterization techniques supplies an explicit model of that noise. Geometric-mean mixing in TPS, Cp stack-up in LFA and contact resistance in D5470 are not random scatter. They are directional, partly predictable biases of the same order as the residuals any model leaves. The project's distinctive contribution is therefore not row count, which will stay in the low hundreds like every comparable dataset, but metadata that lets the method-driven part of the residuals be tested and quantified. The same reasoning reframes the physics fits. A fitted Kapitza resistance one to three orders above the molecular-dynamics floor is best read as a per-paper index of dispersion, porosity and measurement quality, which makes it a useful ML feature, not a material constant.

Two gaps in the literature are open opportunities rather than obstacles. No peer-reviewed study has measured one BN/epoxy specimen by LFA, TPS (isotropic and anisotropic) and D5470, and no BN/AlN–epoxy ML paper reports leave-paper-out performance. A course project that publishes a grouped-validation baseline, even at the honest factor-of-1.5–2 error expected, and proposes or performs a same-specimen method comparison would add more to the field than another random-split R² of 0.98. The weakest links to resolve early are the unverified identities of Chinese instruments such as DRL-III and TC 3000, which affect a large share of rows, and verification of the textbook-form model equations against their original papers before the module is frozen.
