Abstract 
Polymer composites with thermal conductivity are attractive for heat dissipation in electronic devices. 
We prepared epoxy resin-based composite films filled with a polythiophene [poly(3-hexylthiophene-
2,5-diyl)] (P3HT)/graphene complex or aluminum nitride (AlN). The composite films were 
characterized by electron microscopic observation, thermogravimetric analysis, and thermal 
conductivity measurements. The addition of a small content of P3HT/graphene complex (<1 wt%) 
increased both the out-of-plane and in-plane thermal conductivities of the epoxy resin. The addition of 
AlN also increased thermal conductivity, but ≥40 wt% was required to obtain thermal conductivity 
comparable with that of an epoxy resin film doped with 0.9 wt% P3HT/graphene complex. The present 
results illustrate the potential of P3HT/graphene complexes as fillers for improving the thermal 
conductivity of epoxy resins without affecting their insulating properties. 
 
Highlights 
Polythiophene/graphene fillers increased epoxy resin films’ thermal conductivity. 
To raise thermal conductivity of epoxy resin films high AlN content was needed. 
In-plane/out-of-plane epoxy resin films’ thermal conductivities were evaluated. 
Keywords: epoxy resin, carbon nanomaterial, thermal conductivity, filler, graphene, composite,

1 INTRODUCTION 
Modern electronic devices are becoming more integrated and smaller, which renders them increasingly 
ubiquitous and wearable. However, as the power density of electronic devices increases, the quantity 
of heat generated also increases. Thermal management in electronic devices is important because heat 
affects device performance, stability, and lifetime [1]. Heat dissipation using materials with high 
thermal conductivity is a common option for thermal management. Given the needs of wearable and 
portable electronic devices, heat-dissipating materials should be strong, lightweight, inexpensive, and 
insulating [2]. Polymer-based materials are good options in this context. Although polymers typically 
exhibit low thermal conductivity, polymer composites of thermally conductive materials exhibit 
promising thermal conductivity and electrical insulation properties [3-7]. Representative examples 
include polymer composites with metal nitrides [8, 9], metal oxides [3, 10], and carbon nanomaterials 
[11, 12]. However, a high filler content is often required to raise the thermal conductivity of the 
composite, which impairs other properties of the polymer. It should also be noted that the out-of-plane 
(through-plane) conductivity of composites is often lower than that in-plane, and that often one or the 
other has not been reported [7]. Both in-plane and out-of-plane conductivity affect heat dissipation in 
electronic devices. 
Epoxy resins are common thermosetting polymers in electrical devices. Such resins offer many 
advantages including strong adhesion to various materials, high corrosion resistance, excellent 
electrical insulation properties, and easy processing [13]. Unfortunately, the thermal conductivity of 


===== PAGE 5 =====
4 
 
epoxy resins is as low as ~0.2 W/(m·K) [7]. Epoxy-based composites containing a variety of thermally 
conductive materials have been developed to increase thermal conductivity [7, 14-21]. However, high 
contents of fillers were required to achieve high thermal conductivity. 
Graphene has emerged as a promising two-dimensional nanomaterial that exhibits high thermal 
conductivity, mechanical toughness, and gas-barrier performance, as well as useful optical properties 
[22-24]. Over the last decade, much work has focused on preparing graphene and graphene nanosheets 
from graphite [11, 23, 25-30]. However, because of the poor compatibility of graphene with 
commodity polymers, it is still challenging to prepare homogeneous polymer–graphene composites. 
We previously reported the liquid-phase exfoliation of pristine graphite using poly(3-hexylthiophene-
2,5-diyl) (P3HT) as a dispersant [31, 32]. Polythiophene P3HT is a well-studied π-conjugated polymer 
with favorable thermal conductivity and processability [33, 34]. We prepared a P3HT/graphene 
complex without loss of electrical conductivity and dispersed it in an organic solvent [31, 32], which 
implies the potential of P3HT/graphene complexes as thermally conductive fillers. 
Aluminum nitride (AlN) has high thermal conductivity and exhibits insulating properties [8, 35], 
which are promising for thermal management in electric devices. In fact, AlN is already in practical 
use. In the present study, we prepare epoxy resin films containing P3HT/graphene and AlN with the 
aim of developing advanced heat-dissipating materials. The effects of P3HT/graphene and AlN on the 
in-plane and out-of-plane conductivities of the doped epoxy resin films are explored.

2 Experimental 
2.1 Materials 
Graphite flakes were provided by Sekisui Chemical Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Other chemicals were 
purchased from Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd. (Osaka, Japan). Water was deionized (Milli-Q, 
>15 MΩ cm−1) using a Milli-Q Advantage A100 system equipped with an Elix UV 3 system (Millipore, 
Molsheim, France) before use. The epoxy monomer (JER 828) was obtained from Mitsubishi 
Chemical Corporation (Tokyo, Japan). Methyltetrahydrophthalic anhydride (HN-2200) as a hardener 
and 1-cyanoethyl-2-ethyl-4-methylimidazole (2E4MZ-CN) as a catalyst were obtained from Resonac 
Corporation (Tokyo, Japan). AlN with a mean particle size of 1.0 µm was obtained from Tokuyama 
Corporation (Tokyo, Japan). 
2.2 Preparation of P3HT and P3HT/graphene 
 
Figure 1 Synthesis of poly(3-hexylthiophene-2,5-diyl) (P3HT) [36]. 
 
P3HT was synthesized using our previously reported procedure (Figure 1) [36]. Briefly, 2-bromo-3-
hexylthiophene (1.59 mmol) was added at 25 °C to THF (20 mL) containing TMPMgCl·LiCl (1.90 
mmol). After stirring at 25 °C for 3 h, THF (20 mL) and NiCl2(PPh3)IPr (34.3 mg, 0.044 mmol) were 


===== PAGE 7 =====
6 
 
successively added. The resulting mixture was stirred at 25 °C for 1 h. Aqueous hydrochloric acid (0.1 
M, 10 mL) and methanol (10 mL) were added to form a precipitate. The precipitate was isolated by 
filtration and was then washed with n-hexane to obtain a dark purple solid. The solid was dried under 
reduced pressure to yield P3HT (160 mg, 61%). The number-average molecular weight and 
polydispersity index of P3HT were 6,000 and 1.10, respectively, which were estimated by size 
exclusion chromatography using polystyrene as a standard and chloroform as the eluent. The sheet 
resistance of a P3HT film was too high to measure (> 106 kΩ sq−1). 
To prepare the P3HT/graphene complex, chloroform (10 mL) containing graphite flakes (5 mg) was 
ultrasonicated using an ultrasonic cleaner (VS-D100, 31 kHz, AsOne Corporation, Osaka, Japan) at 
25 °C for 1 h. The suspension was mixed with P3HT (5 mg) in chloroform (3 g). The resulting mixture 
was ultrasonicated at 25 °C for 1 or 3 h, followed by evaporation of chloroform at 25 °C for 3 days. 
The sheet resistance of the P3HT/graphene complex was 1.8 ± 0.5 kΩ sq−1 [31]. 
2.3 Preparation of epoxy resin films 
Epoxy monomer, hardener, and catalyst were mixed at a weight ratio of 50/49.8/0.2 for 0.5 h at 
atmospheric pressure, followed by stirring under reduced pressure for 1 h at 25 °C. Filler was added 
to the mixture, which was stirred for 1 h at atmospheric pressure and then mixed under reduced 
pressure for 1 h. The mixture was poured into a cell composed of a pair of aluminum plates with a 
Teflon film (210 µm thick) sandwiched between them as a spacer. The mixture in the cell was cured 
at 120 °C for 2 h to form an epoxy resin film.

2.4 Characterization of epoxy resin films 
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation was carried out using a JEOL 2100F 
transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) operated at 200 kV. Thin films (20–30 nm 
thick) were prepared using an ultramicrotome with a diamond knife. The thin films were placed on a 
copper grid covered with an elastic carbon film (Okenshoji Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) for TEM 
observation. 
The AlN powder and the cross section of the epoxy resin film containing 30 wt% AlN were observed 
using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; JSM-7500F, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) 
operating at an accelerating voltage of 7 kV. Each sample was sputter-coated with an ultrathin osmium 
layer using an osmium coater (Neoc-STB, Meiwafosis Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) prior to FE-SEM 
observation. 
The thermal conductivity of the epoxy resin films was assessed using periodic heating−temperature 
measurements with a Thermowave Analyzer TA3 (Bethel Co., Ltd., Ibaraki, Japan) [37]. Films were 
cut into pieces of ca. 20 × 20 mm and then both surfaces were blackened to enhance heating with 
infrared laser light. The laser output power was set at 1%. In the measurement of the in-plane thermal 
diffusivity, the distance between the heating spots and detection points ranged from 0.5 to 1.0 mm, and 
the frequency of heating was from 0.1 to 0.3 Hz. To measure the out-of-plane thermal diffusivity, the 
frequency ranged from 0.1 to 10 Hz. The thermal diffusivity was measured in five different areas of 
the samples and the mean value was used in the subsequent calculations. Thermal conductivity (κ) was 


===== PAGE 9 =====
8 
 
calculated as κ = αρCp, where ρ is the bulk density and Cp is the specific heat capacity measured by a 
differential scanning calorimeter. The bulk density of epoxy resin films was measured using the sink–
float method with a pycnometer at 30 °C. 
Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed in an air atmosphere with a heating rate of 
10 °C/min in a platinum crucible using a thermal analyzer (Thermoplus EVO 2 TG8121, Rigaku, 
Japan). The AlN in an epoxy resin film was observed by X-ray computed tomography (CT) using a 
high-resolution X-ray microscope (nano3DX, Rigaku). CT scans were conducted at 35 kV and 24 mA 
with a Cu Kα1 (λ = 1.54 Å, 17.5 keV) radiation source and resolution of 0.54 μm per pixel. Electrical 
resistance measurements were performed using a four-point-probe ohmmeter (LorestaAX MCP-T370, 
Nittoseiko Analytech Co., Ltd., Kanagawa, Japan). 
 
3 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
We prepared epoxy resin films containing fillers using a typical curing procedure. The thickness of 
the prepared epoxy films ranged from 200 to 300 µm. Electrical resistance measurements indicated 
that the prepared epoxy films exhibited insulating properties (>106 Ω/cm) even though pristine graphite 
flakes, P3HT, and P3HT/graphene show electrical conductivity [31].

We measured the in-plane and out-of-plane thermal conductivities of epoxy resin films containing 
different types and contents of fillers (Figure 5). The epoxy resin film without any fillers exhibited a 
thermal conductivity of 0.08 W/(m·K) both in-plane and out-of-plane, which was comparable with 
previous reports [38-40]. Adding graphite flakes increased both the in-plane and out-of-plane thermal 
conductivities of the epoxy resin film (Figure 5a). The maximum in-plane thermal conductivity was 
obtained for the epoxy resin film with 0.1 wt% graphite flakes. The out-of-plane thermal conductivity 
of graphite flake-doped films was constant when the graphite flake content exceeded >0.1 wt% and 
was lower than the in-plane thermal conductivity of the films, which indicates surface segregation of 
graphite flakes in the epoxy resin. Hydrophobic compounds and functional groups are likely to 
segregate at an air/polymer interface to minimize surface energy [41, 42]. The hydrophobic nature of 
the graphite flakes would induce their surface segregation in the epoxy resin. Upon adding P3HT to 
the epoxy resin film, the in-plane thermal conductivity increased (Figure 5b). The P3HT contents of 
0.05 wt% and 0.9 wt% gave the films with the highest in-plane thermal conductivity. Although adding 
P3HT also increased the out-of-plane thermal conductivity of the epoxy resin films, it was constant 
when P3HT content was >0.025 wt%. 
When we added the P3HT/graphene complex to the epoxy resin, the in-plane thermal conductivity 


===== PAGE 15 =====
14 
 
of the films varied with the complex content (Figure 5c). Adding 0.1 wt% P3HT/graphene gave the 
highest in-plane thermal conductivity of 0.99 W/(m·K), whereas 0.9 wt% P3HT/graphene resulted in 
an in-plane thermal conductivity of 0.53 W/(m·K). The in-plane thermal conductivity did not increase 
in proportion to the content of graphite flakes, P3HT, and P3HT/graphene of the epoxy resin films. In 
contrast, the out-of-plane thermal conductivity increased with the content of P3HT/graphene for the 
epoxy resin films doped with this filler. A P3HT/graphene complex content of 0.9 wt% afforded an 
out-of-plane thermal conductivity of 0.58 W/(m·K), which was almost the same as the in-plane thermal 
conductivity of this film. The coincidence of the out-of-plane and in-plane thermal conductivities for 
the film with 0.9 wt% P3HT/graphene indicates an isotropic distribution of the P3HT/graphene 
complex in this epoxy resin film. These results also imply the formation of a three-dimensional heat 
transfer network in this composite because of its unique morphology [37]. 
Intriguingly, the in-plane thermal conductivity of the epoxy resin films decreased when the contents 
of graphite flakes, P3HT, and P3HT/graphene exceeded certain thresholds. High contents of these 
fillers might promote their aggregation and affect their surface segregation, which resulted in the 
observed decrease of in-plane thermal conductivity. 
Figure 5d shows the effect of AlN on the thermal conductivity of the epoxy resin films. Both in-
plane and out-of-plane thermal conductivities increased in proportion to the AlN content of the films, 
which is in good agreement with previous reports [16, 43]. The in-plane and out-of-plane thermal 
conductivities were almost equal, implying an isotropic distribution of AlN in the epoxy resin films.

These results are consistent with the X-ray CT observations. An AlN content of 40 wt% resulted in a 
thermal conductivity of ca. 0.5 W/(m·K), whereas a P3HT/graphene content of only 0.9 wt% led to a 
thermal conductivity of >0.5 W/(m·K). 
 
4 CONCLUSIONS 
We prepared insulating epoxy resin composite films containing a P3HT/graphene complex or AlN. 
The out-of-plane and in-plane thermal conductivities of the composites were evaluated. Adding a small 
content of P3HT/graphene complex (<1 wt%) increased both the out-of-plane and in-plane thermal 
conductivities of the epoxy resin. Adding AlN also increased the out-of-plane and in-plane thermal 
conductivities of the epoxy resin films, but ≥40 wt% was required to obtain thermal conductivity 
comparable with that of an epoxy resin film with 0.9 wt% P3HT/graphene complex. These results 
reveal the potential of P3HT/graphene complexes as fillers for improving the thermal conductivity of 
epoxy resins without affecting their insulating properties.
